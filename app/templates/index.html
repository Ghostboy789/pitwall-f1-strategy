The tyre data hides the answer.

Teams pit when a tyre is finished, so the worst part of every wear curve is never recorded. They also put the harder tyre on exactly the stints where wear will be worst.

Read naively, the data says hard tyres wear faster than soft ones, which is backwards. Two corrections, reported side by side, recover the right order without being told it: a two-way fixed-effects comparison of cars at the same moment of the same race, then inverse-probability weights for stints that were cut short.

When the overtaking model says 30%, it happens 30% of the time.

Ranking passes well isn't enough for a model that outputs probabilities: they have to be right at every level.

Every prediction is out-of-fold and split by race, so no race is ever in both training and test.

The strategy model failed its own test.

Before any result existed, a limit was written down: if the optimiser claims to beat real F1 strategists by more than 2 seconds on average, the model is broken, not brilliant.

Real races don't punish strategy the way the simulator does.

The same {{ "{:,}".format(strategy_validation.n_car_races) }} cars in {{ strategy_validation.n_races }} races, measured twice: from real finishing times, and from simulated replays of their exact strategies.

In real races, an extra pit stop and an uneven split of stints cost nothing measurable. The simulator charges for both. Per-lap tyre wear is estimated correctly; what breaks is the step from lap to race, because the simulator runs every car flat out on its fitted wear rate while real drivers manage their tyres.
